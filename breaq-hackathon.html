About the hackathon

The BreaQ Hackathon is the part of BreaQ where you build: teams take on real quantum computing challenges at the CAMPUS Research Institute, with mentors on the floor and prizes at the end. Every level is welcome, from complete beginners to researchers, and the BreaQ Conference follows three weeks later.

What to Expect

Three challenges on real quantum problems, each with a starter kit, mentors on the floor and prizes for its top three teams: 500, 300 and 100 RON. New to quantum? Quantum Foundations is designed for you.

For beginners

Quantum Foundations

Designed for beginners, this track introduces fundamental quantum concepts and quantum algorithms through a series of small, hands‑on exercises. Participants will progress through challenges of increasing difficulty, building their understanding step by step and gaining confidence in designing quantum circuits.

Built with PennyLane

Quantum AI

Quantum AI has the potential to process data in new ways and offer advantages over classical approaches, but noise on near‑term quantum hardware can undermine its practical performance. Participants will use PennyLane to explore a quantum AI pipeline and how it must adapt to realistic, noisy conditions.

Quantum security

Quantum Hacking

Quantum hacking exposes weaknesses in quantum devices, algorithms, and communication protocols. Participants will explore how real‑world imperfections can be exploited, showing that even quantum technologies are not immune to attack.

Last year’s challenges

At the first BreaQ, in November 2025, every team picked one of three challenges:

Quantum machine learning

Entangled Learning

Participants were challenged to develop hybrid quantum–classical neural networks for grading diabetic retinopathy from RetinaMNIST medical images. The task involved designing new architectures and experiments to assess whether quantum components improve predictive performance, reduce the number of trainable parameters, or enable better generalization from limited training data compared with classical models.

Built with Classiq

Quantum Codebreaking

Participants were challenged to implement the Kuperberg sieve algorithm using Classiq, combining quantum operations with classical post‑processing to recover a hidden parameter in the Dihedral Hidden Subgroup Problem. The task explored how quantum algorithms can tackle hard problems relevant to cryptanalysis, with an additional challenge to adapt the implementation for execution on quantum hardware.

Quantum key distribution

Quantum Hacking

Participants were challenged to simulate attacks on quantum key distribution, focusing on intercept–resend attacks against BB84 and Trojan‑horse attacks that exploit information leakage from quantum devices. The task involved measuring how attacks affect security, evaluating detection and defense strategies, and analyzing trade‑offs, with optional extensions covering additional device attacks and adversarial attacks on quantum machine learning models.
